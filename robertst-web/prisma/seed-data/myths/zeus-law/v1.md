Zeus was concerned, news had reached him that people’s faith in the gods was fading away. He called his son Hermes, the emissary, and decided to take a stroll through the scraggly fields of Greece to see for himself. They got to the valley of Phrygia and ran into a small village there, wearing shabby clothes, with their hoods pulled over their heads.

The sunset lingered, though the sun was no longer visible. People were still moving around in the fading light. Some had been ready to wrap up for a while, longing to hear someone say: “That’ll do for today.”

“Winter is close,” a couple of men murmured as they walked away from a field.

Zeus noticed a middle-aged woman milking a cow in a fenced yard behind a house.

“Good evening, milkmaid,” said Zeus. “I’m here in the name of Zeus. May I have some of that milk you’re getting out there?”

She glanced at him for a brief moment before mechanically turning her eyes back to the cow’s nipples. Zeus kept watching her chubby hands move like the hands of a clock. No reply.

“You’ve been ghosted by a milkmaid, ha!” said Hermes.

They tried another house, one with flashes of light coming from within.

“Hello? We’ve come a long way. Could you spare us a corner to rest in for tonight?”

The reply was a puff of breath, followed by darkness.

“Wood prices are through the roof!” Hermes said gently.

They tried several more houses with no success at all. Zeus felt himself withering; memories of better days flooded his mind, along with a cloudy vision of his dear Olympus. His face began to settle into a frown.

“What are those pagans up to?” he asked himself.

On their way out of Phrygia, already past its periphery, a small inn caught their eye, with a chimney that looked like a forgotten lit cigar.

They arrived at the porch. Zeus ran his finger along the windowsill.

“Not even a speck of dust.”

The bench looked brand new. “It reminds me of the one we have in the hall on Olympus,” said Hermes. “But this one’s IKEA.”

As they crossed the porch towards the door, their footsteps made the wood shriek beneath them. Hermes raised his fist, and just before his knuckles reached the door, it opened a crack.

“Who is it?”

“Good evening, sir,” Hermes said gently. “We would really appreciate a meal and a corner to rest our bodies tonight. Would that be possible?”

“Please, hold on for a moment,” the voice said as the crack closed.

They heard whispers passing quickly back and forth.

“…this is a mess!” said one.

“It’s fine…” whispered another. “…coming!”

The door opened wide, releasing a wave of warm light.

“Hello, youngsters, come on in! My name is Philemon.”

“And mine is Baucis,” said the woman beside him.

Baucis asked them to remove their shoes and leave them by the door, next to shelves full of washed dishes reflecting the room’s firelight. Zeus’s eyes followed a row of tools along the wall, each a little longer than the last.

“It didn’t look this spacious from outside!” Hermes said.

Baucis brought wine from their own harvest, cured meats and cheese they had made themselves. Philemon arranged the table.

“This cheese… oh, this cheese. The goat that gave us the milk lived around thirty years. Strongest goat we ever had, and nuts!”

Baucis nodded as she poured the wine.

Philemon continued: “Always jumping and fighting. I’ve never seen so much life in a goat. Always trying to sleep with the other goats, and when that didn’t work, she’d start headbutting the wall of the house. Made a hole in it!”

“Maybe it was Zeus dressed as a goat!” said Hermes, drawing a belly laugh from Philemon.

“We had to repair it twice,” Baucis said.

“A unique goat. I can tell you that,” added Philemon.

Zeus put a piece of the goat’s cheese in his mouth. As he chewed, the story seemed to unfold in it: thirty years of jumping, fighting, unsuccessful seduction and adaptation. The taste was sublime. They spent the rest of the evening joking about better days.

Baucis laid a couple of blankets beside the fire and looked at her guests.

“All right, time to go to bed… Good night,” She headed to a room next door.

“You’re waiting for me, right?” Philemon asked.

No reply. Philemon went right after her.

“They’re a lovely couple, Zeus. They’re old and they don’t have much, but they’ve given us everything tonight. The goat’s cheese was pretty bad though.”

Zeus turned to look at him.

Hermes pulled his blanket over his shoulder.

“…and the blankets are also from IKEA.”

As the fire sank, its light gave way to the moon coming through the window. In the corner of the frame, it caught a little patch of dust and a spiderweb.

When they woke, it was still dark outside. Zeus went to wake the couple.

“Wake up. I am Zeus, and you have to come with us.”

Philemon sat up. Baucis reached for his hand. They held each other tightly and stared at Zeus.

Hermes appeared behind him, carrying coffee for everyone.

“Good morning to our dear hosts! Sorry about the tension, pre-coffee Zeus is very intense. I’m Hermes, by the way.”

“Don’t take anything,” Zeus said. “We need to leave now.”

“Hold on a moment,” said Baucis through clenched teeth. “Prove you are who you claim to be.”

Zeus drained his cup in one gulp and tilted it towards them. As they watched, dark coffee welled up from the bottom with a thin layer of crema on its surface.

“Italian double espresso.”

“Not that dishwater *Americano* thing,” Hermes added.

Zeus drank it again.

“You need to follow us. You were the only ones who treated us properly. Your neighbours will have to pay.”

Philemon and Baucis followed the two gods up the nearest mountain, with nothing more than small talk along the way.

When they reached a clearing overlooking the valley, Zeus stopped.

“Look back.”

They turned. It was still night, but a few lights shone from the houses below.

“Now look at me.”

Everyone looked at him.

“Now look back again.”

Below them lay a dark lake covering the entire valley. Baucis and Philemon could hear their own hearts madly pumping.

“I had to do it,” said Zeus.

Philemon gripped Baucis’s hand tightly, staring at where, moments before, little lighthouses had seemed to shine; both holding on to each other’s heartbeat, both speechless.

“Now, hold on tight.”

Everything around them began to tremble. An island with a marble temple rose from the water, where their little house had stood. The trembling ended as the sunrise joined the party.

“You’re so dramatic! Ha!” said Hermes.

“From now on, this temple belongs to you, as a token of gratitude for your faith in the gods,” said Zeus. “And before I leave, you may ask me for one wish. Whatever you want. Infinite gold, eternal life, eternal beauty, a fishing rod. Whatever you wish.”

Philemon and Baucis looked into each other’s eyes for a brief moment; the wrinkles on their faces shifting slightly in a language the gods couldn’t understand.

“When the time comes, we want to share our last breath,” Philemon said.

Zeus’s frown softened for a moment.

“All right, consider it done! One thing before we leave. Share the story of what happened here with everyone you encounter.”

Zeus and Hermes set off for home. As they waved their final goodbye from a distance, Zeus said to Hermes:

“Those pagans are crazy.”

“They’re just funny!” replied Hermes.

As the years passed and people walked by the temple, Philemon and Baucis told them about the great Zeus, who had appeared at their door dressed as a homeless man and given their home a complete gut renovation.

When the moment came, a coldness began to set in, and they knew right away. Philemon quickly gathered Baucis into his arms, which began to turn into branches.

Legend has it that the temple crumbled under the battering winds; but for a long, long time afterwards, two trees could still be seen there: a proud, strong oak embracing a white-trunked linden full of blossom.
